# Logical Fallacy Reference

For each fallacy: definition, a typical example, how to detect it, and how to fix the argument. Report fallacies in the user's language, giving the standard name (with the English term in parentheses if answering in Chinese).

---

## Formal Fallacies

### 1. Affirming the Consequent
**Definition**: From "If P then Q" and "Q", concluding "P".
**Example**: "If it rained, the ground is wet. The ground is wet, so it rained." (A sprinkler also wets the ground.)
**Detect**: The evidence is the *effect*; the conclusion asserts one particular *cause*.
**Fix**: Treat P as one hypothesis among several; rule out alternative causes of Q.

### 2. Denying the Antecedent
**Definition**: From "If P then Q" and "not P", concluding "not Q".
**Example**: "If you study hard you'll pass. You didn't study hard, so you'll fail." (One can pass by other means.)
**Detect**: The conditional is treated as if it were biconditional ("only if").
**Fix**: Ask whether P is the *only* route to Q; if not, the inference fails.

### 3. Four-Term Fallacy / Equivocation
**Definition**: A syllogism whose middle term shifts meaning between premises.
**Example**: "A feather is light. What is light cannot be dark. Therefore a feather cannot be dark."
**Detect**: The same word carries different senses in different premises.
**Fix**: Define the term once, precisely, and rewrite both premises with that single sense.

## Informal Fallacies

### 4. Ad Hominem
**Definition**: Attacking the person instead of the argument.
**Example**: "You can't trust her climate analysis — she's an activist."
**Detect**: The rebuttal targets motives, character, or identity, not premises or logic.
**Fix**: Evaluate the argument on its merits; bias is a reason for scrutiny, not refutation.

### 5. Straw Man
**Definition**: Refuting a distorted, weaker version of the opponent's position.
**Example**: "They want a moderate speed limit" → "They want to ban driving."
**Detect**: Compare the rebutted claim against the opponent's actual words — is it recognizably the same?
**Fix**: Steel-man instead: restate the strongest version of the position, then respond to that.

### 6. False Dilemma
**Definition**: Presenting two options as exhaustive when more exist.
**Example**: "Either we cut the budget or the company dies."
**Detect**: An "either/or" framing; ask what third options were excluded.
**Fix**: Enumerate the genuine option space before choosing.

### 7. Appeal to Emotion
**Definition**: Substituting emotional force (fear, pity, outrage) for evidence.
**Example**: "Think of the children!" as the entire case for a policy.
**Detect**: Removing the emotional content leaves no logical support for the conclusion.
**Fix**: Emotions can flag stakes; the conclusion still needs independent evidential support.

### 8. Slippery Slope
**Definition**: Claiming a first step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome without showing the mechanism.
**Example**: "Allow remote Fridays and soon nobody will ever come to the office."
**Detect**: A causal chain asserted without probability or mechanism for each link.
**Fix**: Assess each link separately; identify where the chain can realistically be stopped.

### 9. Begging the Question (Circular Reasoning)
**Definition**: The conclusion is smuggled into the premises.
**Example**: "The Bible is true because it says so."
**Detect**: Ask what independent support each premise has; if a premise presupposes the conclusion, it's circular.
**Fix**: Find premises acceptable to someone who does not already accept the conclusion.

### 10. Red Herring
**Definition**: Diverting to an irrelevant issue to dodge the original question.
**Example**: "Yes, the product has bugs — but our competitors' marketing is dishonest."
**Detect**: Trace whether the response actually bears on the original claim.
**Fix**: Name the diversion and return to the question at issue.

### 11. Appeal to Authority (Inappropriate)
**Definition**: Citing an authority outside their domain of expertise, or as a substitute for available evidence.
**Example**: "A Nobel physicist says this diet works."
**Detect**: Check whether the authority's expertise matches the claim's domain, and whether experts in that domain agree.
**Fix**: Cite domain-relevant expert consensus, and prefer the underlying evidence when it can be examined directly.

### 12. Hasty Generalization
**Definition**: Generalizing from a sample that is too small or unrepresentative.
**Example**: "Two of my friends got food poisoning there — that restaurant is dangerous."
**Detect**: Ask about sample size and how the sample was selected.
**Fix**: Qualify the conclusion, or gather a larger, representative sample.

### 13. Weak Analogy
**Definition**: Arguing from a comparison whose similarities are irrelevant, or whose differences are critical.
**Example**: "Employees are like family, so we should never let anyone go."
**Detect**: List the similarities and differences; check whether the similarities actually drive the conclusion.
**Fix**: Restrict the analogy to the respects in which it genuinely holds.

### 14. Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
**Definition**: Inferring causation from mere temporal sequence.
**Example**: "We launched the ad campaign and sales rose, so the campaign caused it."
**Detect**: The only causal evidence is "B happened after A."
**Fix**: Look for controls, counterfactuals, or a mechanism; consider confounders and seasonality.

### 15. Cherry-Picking (Selective Evidence)
**Definition**: Presenting only the evidence that supports the conclusion.
**Example**: Quoting the one quarter of growth while omitting three quarters of decline.
**Detect**: Ask what the full dataset or literature shows; look for the evidence that was left out.
**Fix**: Survey the total evidence base before concluding; report contrary findings.

### 16. Appeal to Ignorance
**Definition**: Treating absence of evidence as proof ("no one has disproven it, so it's true").
**Example**: "No study shows this additive is harmful, so it's safe."
**Detect**: The conclusion rests on what is *not* known.
**Fix**: State the claim's status honestly: unproven, with the burden of proof on the asserter.

### 17. Tu Quoque ("You Too")
**Definition**: Deflecting criticism by pointing to the critic's own inconsistency.
**Example**: "You say smoking is unhealthy, but you smoke."
**Detect**: The response addresses the critic's behavior, not the claim's truth.
**Fix**: Hypocrisy is a separate issue; evaluate the claim independently.
